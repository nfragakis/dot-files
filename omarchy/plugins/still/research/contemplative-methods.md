# Contemplative breathing candidates for Still

## Recommendation in brief

Add the first four as gentle, no-retention presets; consider the fifth as an
explicitly Buddhist, opt-in contemplative practice. Default each to **Medium ·
2 minutes**. The descriptions below deliberately distinguish traditional
framing from measured effects. A two-minute app session is much shorter than
most studies, so the UI should not promise treatment, durable benefit, or the
traditional texts' medical and supernatural claims.

| Candidate | Two-minute Medium pacing | Suggested UI benefit copy | Evidence fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nadi Shodhana | 4 s in left · 6 s out right · 4 s in right · 6 s out left; 6 rounds | “Alternate sides to steady attention and slow the breath.” | Good candidate; clinical studies used longer sessions |
| Bhramari | 4 s nasal inhale · 6 s gentle humming exhale; 12 rounds | “A humming exhale gives the mind a sound and vibration to rest on.” | Good candidate; acute physiology is mixed |
| Ujjayi | 5 s nasal inhale · 5 s gently whispered exhale; 12 rounds | “A soft ocean-like breath makes attention tangible.” | Good experiential candidate; no evidence it outperforms ordinary slow breathing |
| Sitali | 4 s inhale through rolled tongue · 6 s nasal exhale; 12 rounds | “A sensory mouth-inhale practice traditionally associated with coolness and composure.” | Optional; do not claim that it lowers body temperature |
| Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ recitation | 4 s easy inhale · about 6 s resonant recitation/exhale; 12 rounds | “A compassion mantra carried on a slow, resonant breath.” | Opt-in and tradition-labelled; small study supports the pacing, not therapeutic claims |

These timings are conservative **app adaptations**, not reconstructions of the
classical practices or the exact protocols used in every cited experiment.
Classical haṭha instructions commonly add long retentions and muscular “locks”;
Still should not add either without expert teaching.

## 1. Nadi Shodhana / alternate-nostril breathing

**Traditional frame.** The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* describes slow breathing
through one nostril and out through the other, then reversing sides. Its system
frames this as purification of subtle channels (*nāḍī*). Treat that as the
tradition's contemplative model, not an anatomical claim. The same text embeds
the practice in retention and teacher-led progression; Still's beginner version
should omit retention. [Pancham Sinh's 1914 translation, chapter
2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hatha_Yoga_Pradipika/2)

**What has actually been measured.** In a randomized three-condition crossover
study of 15 healthy, yoga-experienced young men, 18 minutes of alternate-nostril
breathing reduced systolic and mean arterial pressure and shortened completion
time on a vigilance task. Sitting quietly also improved task time, and the
sample was small and narrow, so “supports focus” would be too strong; “steady
attention” is appropriately experiential. [Telles et al., 2017, primary
study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5755948/). A separate randomized
study in 170 medicated patients with hypertension reported lower blood pressure
and heart rate after 10 minutes twice daily for five days, but that dose and
population do not justify a claim for a two-minute session. [Kalaivani et al.,
2019, primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6691618/)

**Implementation.** A full round is left inhale → right exhale → right inhale
→ left exhale. Start with the left side, give explicit nostril/hand cues, and
finish the current full round when time expires. No breath holds.

**Contraindications/cautions.** Skip nostril manipulation when either side is
blocked, irritated, bleeding, or recovering from nasal surgery. Stop if it
causes air hunger, dizziness, or anxiety. People taking blood-pressure medicine
or prone to low blood pressure should regard it as a contemplative exercise,
not a way to adjust treatment.

## 2. Bhramari / humming-bee breath

**Traditional frame.** The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* describes an audible
bee-like inhalation and a slow, humming exhalation, associating the sound with
absorbed or ecstatic attention. Still can preserve the sound-focus while using
a gentle, silent nasal inhale rather than the text's forceful version.
[Pancham Sinh translation, Bhramari section](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hatha_Yoga_Pradipika/2#The_Bhramari)

**What has actually been measured.** Humming increased measured nasal nitric
oxide about 15-fold versus a quiet exhale in ten healthy participants because
oscillating airflow increased gas exchange between the nose and sinuses. The
authors explicitly said that therapeutic effects remained to be investigated;
the result is evidence for sinus ventilation, not a general health promise.
[Weitzberg & Lundberg, 2002, primary physiological
study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12119224/). Evidence about immediate
cardiovascular effects is not uniformly “calming”: beat-to-beat monitoring in
20 healthy adults found temporary rises in diastolic pressure, mean arterial
pressure, heart rate, and peripheral resistance during five minutes of
Bhramari, which mostly returned toward baseline afterward. [Nivethitha et al.,
2021, primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8191218/)

**Implementation.** Inhale easily through the nose for 4 seconds, then hum a
comfortable closed-mouth “mmm” for 6 seconds. Keep pitch and volume easy. Do not
instruct users to plug their ears, bear down, fill maximally, or prolong the hum
past comfort.

**Contraindications/cautions.** Skip during an active ear or sinus infection, or
when humming worsens headache, facial pressure, tinnitus, or breathing. Because
acute cardiovascular findings are mixed, avoid copy such as “lowers blood
pressure” or “activates the vagus nerve.”

## 3. Ujjayi / gentle ocean breath

**Traditional frame.** The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* describes narrowing the
laryngeal passage so the breath is audible in the throat. It traditionally adds
retention and a one-nostril exhale; the proposed Still version is a mild modern
adaptation without either. [Pancham Sinh translation, Ujjayi
section](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hatha_Yoga_Pradipika/2#Ujjayi)

**What has actually been measured.** A within-subject study of 17 yoga-naive
healthy adults compared several patterns at six breaths per minute. Slow
breathing improved baroreflex sensitivity, but ordinary 5-in/5-out breathing
performed best overall. Adding Ujjayi increased respiratory effort and reduced
some of the advantage; constricting on both inhale and exhale did not
significantly improve baroreflex sensitivity from baseline. [Mason et al.,
2013, primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655580/)

**Implementation.** Use a normal 5-second nasal inhale, then a 5-second nasal
exhale with only enough throat narrowing to make a soft “ocean” whisper. This
exhale-only version avoids the higher effort seen with constriction in both
directions. The value proposition is an audible attention anchor, not superior
cardiovascular regulation.

**Contraindications/cautions.** The breath must remain quiet and effortless.
Return to ordinary breathing for throat pain, coughing, wheeze, chest tightness,
air hunger, or light-headedness. Do not coach a forceful glottal closure or
Valsalva-like strain.

## 4. Sitali / tongue-curling breath

**Traditional frame.** The *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* instructs inhaling through
a slightly protruded tongue and exhaling slowly through the nose, and places the
method among practices traditionally associated with coolness. Its premodern
cure claims should be presented only as history. [Pancham Sinh translation,
Sitali section](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hatha_Yoga_Pradipika/2#Sitali)

**What has actually been measured.** In a randomized wait-list trial of 60
medicated adults with pre-hypertension or stage-I hypertension, a *combined*
program of Sitali and Sitkari (10 minutes each daily for 30 days) reduced
systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, and heart rate. Because both methods
were bundled, the effect cannot be assigned to Sitali alone, and it says little
about an acute two-minute session. [Shetty et al., 2017, primary
trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6438091/). In a randomized-order
physiology study of 17 healthy men, 18 minutes of Sitali or Sitkari increased
surface temperature and oxygen use during practice; the authors concluded that
their data did **not** support literal body cooling. [Telles et al., 2020,
primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6977599/)

**Implementation.** Curl the tongue into a tube, inhale gently through it for 4
seconds, close the mouth, and exhale through the nose for 6 seconds. Users who
cannot roll their tongue can skip the method; substituting clenched-teeth
Sitkari is a distinct technique and should be named separately.

**Contraindications/cautions.** Avoid in polluted, smoky, very cold, or very dry
air because the inhale bypasses normal nasal filtering and conditioning. Skip
if it irritates sensitive teeth, mouth, or airways. Describe “cooling” as the
traditional name/sensation, never as a measured fall in body temperature.

## 5. Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ / breath-linked compassion mantra

**Traditional frame.** This is a Buddhist mantra of Avalokiteśvara, the
bodhisattva associated with compassion—not a generic “yoga sound.” The
*Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra* is an early scriptural source for the six-syllable
mantra. If Still includes it, retain its name, diacritics or a readable
transliteration, and compassion context; also offer secular humming separately
rather than relabelling the mantra. [84000's translation and introduction to
*The Basket's Display*](https://84000.co/translation/toh116)

**What has actually been measured.** In a comparative within-subject study of
23 healthy adults, resonant recitation of *oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ* naturally settled
near six cycles per minute and increased measured baroreflex sensitivity. The
sample was small, participants were briefly taught by a yoga teacher, and the
study measured acute physiology rather than compassion or mental-health
outcomes. [Bernardi et al., 2001, primary
study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC61046/)

**Implementation.** Prompt an easy inhale, then one unhurried, resonant
recitation carried on the exhale; allow any remaining air to leave comfortably.
Use the 4/6 cue as a loose guide rather than forcing syllables into exact slots.
An optional note can invite the user to recollect compassion, while making no
religious or health claim.

**Contraindications/cautions.** Use a conversational pitch and volume. Stop for
vocal strain, throat discomfort, dizziness, or distress. Users should be free
to choose Bhramari/humming instead of a sacred formula.

## Shared safety and copy rules

- Keep all five beginner presets **gentle, seated, no-retention, and
  non-hyperventilatory**. The classical source itself counsels gradual practice
  and warns against force. [*Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā*, chapter 2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hatha_Yoga_Pradipika/2)
- Show once per session: “Breathe comfortably; smaller breaths are fine. Stop
  for dizziness, tingling, chest pain, faintness, unusual shortness of breath,
  or rising panic.” Never practice while driving, standing in a risky place, or
  in or near water.
- Users with significant heart or lung disease, uncontrolled blood pressure,
  recurrent fainting, seizure disorders, pregnancy complications, or panic
  triggered by breath control should ask a clinician before structured
  breathwork. This is conservative product guidance, not a claim that every
  gentle method is known to be dangerous in those groups.
- Benefits copy should use “may,” “can offer,” or descriptions of the immediate
  task (“sound to rest on”). Avoid “detox,” “balances the hemispheres,” “boosts
  oxygen,” “stimulates the vagus,” disease-treatment claims, and the classical
  texts' supernatural physiology as literal science.
- The evidence base is mostly small, often unblinded, and heterogeneous. No
  cited study establishes that a two-minute session produces durable clinical
  benefit.
