# Scientific breathwork candidates for Still

Research note, 2026-08-14. These are wellness exercises, not treatments. The evidence is mostly small studies of healthy adults, and an app's two-minute session should not be presented as equivalent to a trial that used five or fifteen minutes, coaching, or weeks of practice.

## 1. Cyclic sigh

- **Still phases:** `[["inhale", 2.5], ["sip", 1], ["exhale", 6.5]]`
- **User cue:** “Slow inhale, small second sip, then a gentle long exhale.” Prefer nasal inhales; exhale through the nose or mouth.
- **Plausible benefit:** best-supported short calming option here. In a remote randomized trial (111 enrolled), five minutes daily for 28 days produced a larger rise in positive affect and a larger fall in resting respiratory rate than five minutes of mindfulness. It did **not** outperform mindfulness for state anxiety, negative affect, HRV, heart rate, or sleep. [[Balban et al., 2023]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)
- **Evidence/timing caveat:** the study prescribed the sequence, not fixed seconds. `2.5 / 1 / 6.5` is a comfortable app translation, not a validated ratio. The studied dose was five minutes, so describe a two-minute session as a brief reset rather than promising the trial's month-long effects.

## 2. Coherent slow breathing (generic resonance pacing)

- **Still phases:** `[["inhale", 5.45], ["exhale", 5.45]]` (about 5.5 breaths/min). A simpler display can round to `5.5 / 5.5`.
- **User cue:** “Breathe quietly and evenly; keep the breath smaller than a full lungful.”
- **Plausible benefit:** may acutely increase breathing-linked HRV and relaxation. In a 47-person counterbalanced experiment, each two-minute slow-breathing condition increased reported relaxation; 5.5 breaths/min with equal inhale/exhale produced the largest SDNN and low-frequency HRV among the four tested patterns. [[Lin, Tai & Fan, 2014]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24380741/)
- **Naming caveat:** true *resonance-frequency* breathing is individualized, commonly between about 4.5 and 7 breaths/min, using ECG/HRV feedback. A randomized 95-person study found 15 minutes at the individually measured frequency improved positive mood and some HRV/BP-reactivity measures versus nearby-rate and quiet-sitting controls. Without measurement, label Still's preset **Coherent** or **Slow even**, not personalized “resonance.” [[Steffen et al., 2017]](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2017.00222)

## 3. Extended exhale

- **Still phases:** `[["inhale", 4], ["exhale", 6]]`
- **User cue:** “Easy inhale; let the exhale be longer, smooth, and unforced.”
- **Plausible benefit:** may feel relaxing and can be useful when the user still needs to think or work. The 47-person study above tested a 4:6 inhale/exhale ratio at six breaths/min; all tested slow patterns increased relaxation from baseline, although the equal 5.5-breath/min pattern produced the strongest HRV result. A separate 30-person counterbalanced stress experiment compared tactical/box breathing with a more extreme 2:8 prolonged exhale: box breathing lowered physiological arousal more, while prolonged exhale preserved Stroop-task performance better; subjective effects did not differ. Evidence therefore supports offering the pattern, not claiming that a long exhale is universally superior. [[Lin, Tai & Fan, 2014]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24380741/) [[Röttger et al., 2021]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-020-09485-w)
- **Timing caveat:** `4 / 6` is the gentler product choice. Do not default to `2 / 8`; it is more likely to create air hunger and was tested in a small lab sample.

## 4. Box breathing

- **Still phases:** `[["inhale", 4], ["holdIn", 4], ["exhale", 4], ["holdOut", 4]]`
- **User cue:** “Four equal sides; shorten every side if any hold feels strained.”
- **Plausible benefit:** may provide a structured attentional anchor and lower acute physiological arousal. In the Balban trial, five minutes of daily box breathing was associated with pre/post improvements in positive affect, negative affect, and state anxiety, but it was not significantly better than mindfulness; cyclic sighing was the stronger mood result. In the 30-person Röttger study, tactical/box breathing lowered physiological arousal more than prolonged exhalation during a stress task, but task performance was better with prolonged exhalation. [[Balban et al., 2023]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895) [[Röttger et al., 2021]](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-020-09485-w)
- **Safety/timing caveat:** the Balban protocol scaled equal sides from 3–10 seconds and explicitly told participants to reduce them rather than strain. Four seconds is a sensible beginner ceiling, not a magic number. Offer a no-hold alternative beside it.

## 5. Diaphragmatic (belly) breathing

- **Still phases:** reuse a gentle slow pattern such as `[["inhale", 5], ["exhale", 5]]`; the defining feature is the body cue, not a unique ratio.
- **User cue:** “Let the lower ribs and belly expand; keep shoulders quiet; do not gulp a maximum breath.”
- **Plausible benefit:** repeated coached practice may reduce negative affect and cortisol and improve sustained attention. A 40-person pilot controlled study used 20 coached 15-minute sessions across eight weeks, averaging four breaths/min, and reported those changes versus a control group. This does not establish that a single two-minute session has the same effects. [[Ma et al., 2017]](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00874)
- **Implementation note:** treat “belly breathing” as an instruction overlay or beginner technique. The study did not specify an inhale/exhale ratio that Still should copy, and four breaths/min is unusually slow without coaching.

## Safety copy and product guardrails

- Default to **gentle**, not maximally deep, breaths. Do not force duration or volume. Stop and return to normal breathing for dizziness, light-headedness, tingling, unusual breathlessness, pain, or distress; seek medical care for chest pain, fainting, or severe/persistent symptoms. NHS respiratory guidance similarly advises comfortable, unforced pacing and stopping for dizziness or light-headedness. [[Ashford & St Peter's NHS]](https://www.asph.nhs.uk/nervous-system-regulation) [[Cambridge University Hospitals NHS]](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/breathing-exercises-in-the-treatment-of-hyperventilation/)
- Do sessions seated or lying somewhere safe. Never practice breath retention or rapid/high-ventilation breathing while driving, standing at height, bathing, or in/near water.
- Make holds optional and immediately skippable. People who are pregnant or who have cardiovascular/respiratory disease, panic triggered by air hunger, or a history of fainting or seizures should ask a clinician before using hold- or hyperventilation-heavy methods. This is conservative product guidance because the cited trials largely studied screened healthy adults, not proof of universal contraindications.
- Do **not** make cyclic hyperventilation plus retention a normal/default wellness preset. If retained as an advanced mode, give it a separate explicit warning and never-water rule: hyperventilation before extended underwater breath-holding can delay the urge to breathe and cause hypoxic blackout. [[American Red Cross safety guidance]](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/articles/shallow-water-hypoxic-blackout)

## Suggested product ordering

For a default **Medium · 2 min** session, use **Coherent slow breathing**: the two-minute duration directly matches the short conditions in Lin et al. Offer **Cyclic sigh** and **Extended exhale** as calmer alternatives, **Box** as a hold-based focus option, and **Diaphragmatic** as the beginner mechanics lesson. Phrase benefits as “may help” or “associated with,” never as disease treatment or guaranteed nervous-system effects.
